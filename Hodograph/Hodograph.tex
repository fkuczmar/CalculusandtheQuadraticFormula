\begin{abstract}
A comparison of William Hamilton's and Richard Feynman's proof that planetary orbits are conic sections.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

Calculus students learn to sketch a graph of a function's derivative from the graph of the function. Later they sketch graphs of anti-derivatives thinking about signed area instead of slopes. But few ever sketch the derivative of a motion from a sketch of the motion itself.

It's easy enough to draw the path of a motion and to suggest movement along the path by plotting points or position vectors at equally-spaced time intervals. Students use this information to sketch velocity vectors along the path, drawing them with their tails attached to the tips of the position vectors.

But the velocity vectors are themselves the position vectors of another curve. This curve is called the \emph{hodograph} (from the Greek \emph{hodo}, way), but it's just a way to suggest the derivative of the motion. Like all position vectors, we draw these velocity vectors with their tails pinned at a common origin $O$, and their tips sweep out the hodograph. Drawing pinned velocity vectors at equal time intervals gives some sense of the derivative of the motion along the hodograph, ie. of the acceleration.

For uniform circular motion, for example, the hodograph is a circle centered at the common origin with radius equal to the motion's speed. Since the velocity vector turns at a constant rate, we see at once that its acceleration has constant magntitude. And the hodograph makes it clear at a glance (see Figure ?) that the acceleration vector points directly opposite the position vector.

%The hodogarph of a cycloidal motion is also circular. 
Many other motions have circular hodographs. As a point $P$, fixed relative to a circle rolling on a line, traces a general cycloid, the point $P^*$ at the tip of the pinned velocity vector moves along a circle. When $P$ is on the circumference of the rolling circle, the origin of the pinned velocity vectors lies on the hodograph (a full circle). .... 

%A motion traced by a point $P$ on the circumference of a circle rolling along a line at a constant speed traces a cycloid. But the point $P^*$ at the tip of the pinned velocity vector sweeps out a circle. Now the origin lies \emph{on} the hodograph, not at its center.

The hodograph of any constant-speed curve lies on a circle centered at the origin with a radius equal to the speed. This shows the derivative of the motion along the hodograph, ie. the acceleration, is perpendicular to the velocity vector. The acceleration vector is longer where the motion's path changes direction faster and it's the magnitude of this acceleration vector divided by the constant speed that gives the curvature of the motion's path.

William R. Hamilton is credited with introducing the hodograph in a 1847 paper, \emph{The Hodograph, or a New Method of Expressing in Symbolic Language the Newtonian Law of Attraction},where he uses it to prove \emph{geometrically} that planetary orbits are conic sections. He starts by claiming without proof that the position vector of a motion driven by a central force (ie. one with acceleration vectors all pointing directly toward or away from a common origin) sweeps out area at a constant rate. Newton gave a geometric proof of this claim in Proposition I of his \emph{Principia}, but the modern proof is quick. The key is to recognize that for any motion, the magnitude $|{\bf r}\times {\bf v}|$ of the vector product of the position and velocity vectors (with SI units $\text{m}^2/\text{sec}$) measures twice the rate at which the position vector sweeps out area (see Figure ??). Differentiating the vector product
\[
        {\bf L} = {\bf r} \times {\bf v}
\]
with respect to time shows that
\[
  \frac{d{\bf L}}{dt} = {\bf r}\times {\bf a}
\]
and proves the claim about the area swept by a central force. It's also quick work from here to show the trajectory of a central-force motion lies in a plane. We skip this.

Hamilton and Feynman each start their proofs by showing the hodograph of a planetary motion is a circular arc. Hamilton does this twice, each time by proving the hodograph has constant curvature. We'll start with the second proof (it's easier) after a brief review of curvature.

\section*{Hamilton's hodograph, part I}

I like to think about the curvature of a plane curve dynamically, as if driving a car along a winding road. At some instant, for example, your speed might be $v = 20$ meters/sec while you car is turning at the rate of $\omega = 1/4$ rad/sec. Then at this particular spot the road has curvature 
\[
    \kappa = \frac{\omega}{v} = \frac{1/4\text{ rad./sec}}{20 \text{ meters/sec}} = \frac{1}{80} \frac{\text{ rad}}{\text{meter}}.
\]
The curvature is independent of our speed. It measures the rate at which the road changes direction with respect to distance as measured along the road and as recorded by a car's odometer.  %The radius of curvature is a property of the road itself.

The radius of curvature $\rho$ is the reciprocal of the curvarure and equals $80$ meters at our spot in the road. Letting $s$ be the car's odometer reading and $t$ time (measured in seconds), the radius of curvature is
\[
    \rho =  \frac{ds}{dt} \cdot \frac{1}{\omega} = \frac{ds}{d\theta},
\]
where $d\theta = \omega \, dt$ is the differential angle through which the car's velocity turns during the time interval $dt$.

\section*{The hodograph, Feynman}

Feynman starts by proving Newton's Proposition 1, the position vector of a central-force motion sweeps equal areas in equal times. 
More on this later.


\emph{Feynman: Now the dynamical law is that there are equal changes in velocity, no - that the changes in velocity vary inversely as the square of the distance from the sun - that is, the changes of velocity in equal times. Another way of saying the same thing is that equal changes of velocity will occupy times proportional to the square of the distance. It's the same thing. If I take more time, I get more change in velocity, and, although they are falling for for equal times inversely as the square, if I make my times proportional to the square of the distance, then the changes in velocity will be equal. Or the dynamical law is: eqaul changes in velocity occur in times proportional to the square of the distance.} 

{\bf Interpretation:} By a \emph{change in velocity} (a vector) Feynman must mean the magnitude of that change (a scalar), so that for an inverse-square motion, 
\[
    | \Delta {\bf v} | = |{\bf a}| \Delta t = \frac{k}{r^2} \Delta t
\]
and 
\[
   \Delta t = \frac{r^2}{k} | \Delta {\bf v}|.
\]
That is, \emph{equal changes in velocity occur in times proportional to the square of the distance.}

\emph{Feynman: But look, equal angles were times proportional to the square of the distance. And so we have the conclusion, from the law of gravitation, that equal changes in velocity will occur in equal angles in the orbit. That's the central core from which all will be deduced - that equal changes in velocity occur when the orbit is moving through equal angles.
}

{\bf Interpretation:} From Newton's Proposition 1,
\[
  \Delta A = \frac{r^2}{2} \Delta \theta = K \Delta t ,
\]
and so
\[
  \Delta t = \frac{r^2}{2K} \Delta \theta.
\]
 
That is, \emph{equal angles are in time proportional to the square of the distance.}

So as the position vector turns through the angle $\Delta \theta$, the magnitude $|\Delta v|$ of the change in the velocity (ie. the distance along the hodograph) is
\[
    |\Delta v| = |{\bf a}|\, \Delta t = \frac{k}{r^2}\, \Delta t = \frac{k}{r^2} \left(  \frac{r^2 \Delta \theta}{2K}\right) =\frac{k}{2K}\, \Delta \theta.
\]
That is, \emph{equal changes in velocity occur when the orbit is moving through equal angles.}

Like Hamilton, he next proves the hodograph is a circle, assuming a closed orbit. His proof is essentially identical to Hamilton's although he does not mention curvature explicitly. His idea is to plot the velocity vectors in the hodograph at small equal angles $\Delta \theta$ around the orbit. As the position vector of the orbit turns through the small angle $\Delta \theta$, it sweeps out (unequal) areas $\Delta A = r^2 \Delta \theta/2$, proportional to $r^2$. But for a central force motion the areas $\Delta A = k_1\Delta t$ are also proportional to the times $\Delta t$ over which they are traversed. So the time $\Delta t$ for the position vector to turn through the angle $\Delta \theta$ is proportional to $r^2$. And so for an inverse-square motion ${\bf a}=k{\bf r}/r^3$, the arc lengths 
\[
 \Delta s = |{\bf a}|\, \Delta t = \frac{k}{r^2} \cdot \frac{r^2 \Delta \theta}{2k_1} = \left( \frac{k}{2k_1} \right)\Delta \theta
\]
along the hodograph are proportional to $\Delta \theta$.

 

\section*{Hamilton's hodograph, Part II}

From the introduction, 

\emph{This hodographic curve is even more immediately connected than the orbit with the \emph{forces} which act upon the body, or with the varying resultant of these forces, for the tangent to the hodograph is always parallel to the direction of the resultant; and if th element of the hodograph be divided by the element of the time, the quotient of this division represents (to the usual units) the intensity of the same resultant force; so that the whole accelerating force which acts on the body at any one instant is represented, both in direction and magnitude, by the element of the hodograph divided by the element of time. We have also the general proportion, that the \emph{force is to the velocity, in any varied motion of a point, as the element of the hodograph is to the corresponding element of the orbit.}}

{\bf Interpretation}

\emph{These general remarks respecting varied motion, under the influence of \emph{any} accelerating forces whatever, having been premised, let it be now supposed that the force is constantly directed toward some one \emph{fixed point} or \emph{center}, which it will then be nature to choose for the origin of the vectors of the hodograph. The straight lines drawn to the moving body from the center of force being called, in a like manner, the vectors of the orbit, or the \emph{vectors of position} of the body, we see that each vector of position is now parallel to the tangent of the hodograph drawn at the extremity of the vector of velocity, as the latter vector was seen to be parallel to the tangent of the orbit, drawn at the extremity of the vector of position; so that the two vectors, and the two tangents drawn at their extremities, enclose at each moment a \emph{parallelogram}, of which it is easily seen that the \emph{plane and area are consant}, although its position and its shape are generally variable from one moment to another, in the motion thus performed under the influence of a central force. If therefore, this constant area be given, and if either the hodograph or the orbit be known, the other of these two curves can be deduced, by a simple and uniform process, of which account the two curves themselves may therefore be called \emph{reciprocal hodographs}.}

\emph{The opposite angles of a parallelogram being equal, it is evident, that if the central force be attractive, any one vector of position is inclined to the next following element of the orbit at the same angle as that at which the corresponding vector of velocity in inclined to the next preceeding element of the hodograph. Also, if from either extremity of any small element of the curve, a chord of the circle which osculates to that curve along the element be drawn and bisected, the element subtends, at the middle point of this chord, an angle equal to the angle between the two tangents drawn at the extremities of the element; that is, here, if the curve be the hodograph, to the angle between the two near vectors of position, which are parallel to the two extreme tangents of its element. We have, therefore, two small and similar triangles, from which results the following proposition, that \emph{the half chord of curvature of the hodograph} (passing through, or tending toward the fixed center of force) \emph{is to the radius vector of the orbit} as the element of the hodograph is to the element of the orbit, that is, by what was lately seen, \emph{as the force is to the velocity.}\footnote{By an exactly similar reasoning, the following know proposition may be proved anew, namely, that the force is to the velocity as that velocity is to the half chord of curvature of the orbit, whatever the law of central force may be.}}
